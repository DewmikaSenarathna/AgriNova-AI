## How to Read This Report

This report looks at the **most serious, real-world problems in agriculture today** not opinions from forums or marketing blogs, but findings from the organizations that actually measure global food and farming systems: the **Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO)**, the **World Bank**, the **Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC)**, the **USDA**, the **OECD** and peer-reviewed scientific journals (*Nature*, *Scientific Reports*, *Science*).

Each section explains a problem in **simple English first**, then goes **deeper** into the data, causes and consequences the way a professional briefing document would. At the end, you'll find a **summary table**, a **root-cause map** and **references**.

## Executive Summary

Modern agriculture is not struggling because of one single problem, it is being squeezed from many directions at once. In simple terms: **the land is getting weaker, the water is running out, the climate is becoming unpredictable, the workers are getting older and fewer, the helpers (bees and insects) are disappearing and the money farmers need is hard to get.** Each of these problems makes the others worse.

The seven core problems covered in this report are:

1. **Land degradation** - soil is losing its ability to grow food
2. **Water scarcity** - the world is running short of usable freshwater
3. **Climate change** - heat, drought and extreme weather are cutting yields
4. **Pests, diseases and invasive species** - spreading faster due to warming
5. **Pollinator decline** - bees and wild insects are dying off, threatening nutrition
6. **Labor shortages and an aging farming workforce** - fewer young people are farming
7. **Financial exclusion and post-harvest losses** - smallholder farmers lose money and food before it even reaches a market

## Problem 1: Land Degradation - The Ground Beneath Farming Is Wearing Out

### Simple explanation
Soil is like a bank account. Every time you grow crops, you take something out of it nutrients, structure, water-holding capacity. If you don't put anything back (through rest, organic matter or careful management), the "account" runs low. That's land degradation: soil that has been worn down by farming practices, erosion and poor management until it can no longer produce as much food as before.

### The deeper picture
FAO's flagship 2025 report, *The State of Food and Agriculture*, focused entirely on this problem because it has become so severe. <cite index="1-1">Approximately 1.7 billion people worldwide live in areas experiencing yield losses linked to human-induced land degradation, with Southern Asia and Eastern Asia containing the largest affected populations due to high population density and long-term accumulated damage.</cite>

FAO created a new measurement called the **"Land Degradation Debt"** - a way of comparing today's soil health to what it would naturally be without human damage. <cite index="7-1">This model, built using machine learning, found a 30% loss in tree cover, a 20% loss in biomass carbon and a fourfold increase in soil erosion caused directly by human activity.</cite> <cite index="7-1">About 20% of the world's cropland now shows declining productivity because of erosion and loss of organic carbon, especially across Asia, Africa and Latin America.</cite>

A particularly troubling finding: <cite index="7-1">the largest farms - the top 1% of all farms - control over 70% of the world's agricultural land and their intensive practices such as monocropping and heavy fertilizer use often accelerate degradation, even though these same large farms have more resources available for restoration.</cite> Meanwhile, degradation is often hidden on smaller farms because <cite index="1-1">India, which holds the world's largest share of agricultural landholdings (29.4% of the global total), has a typical farm size of only half a hectare and farmers there compensate for degraded soil by using more chemical fertilizer - masking the underlying damage while keeping yields temporarily stable.</cite>

The result of this soil decline isn't only lower yields - it is also a climate problem. <cite index="7-1">Degraded soils release significant greenhouse gases, which worsens climate change and undermines the world's goal of achieving "land degradation neutrality" under the Sustainable Development Goals.</cite> Between 1992 and 2015, over 60 million hectares of farmland were abandoned worldwide, mostly across Eastern Europe, Central Asia and South America, largely because yields fell too low to justify continued farming.

### Why this matters
Land degradation is a slow-moving crisis - it doesn't make headlines the way a flood does, but it quietly reduces the amount of food the planet can produce, year after year, in the places that can least afford it.

## Problem 2: Water Scarcity - Running Out of the One Thing Farming Cannot Do Without

### Simple explanation
Farming needs water more than almost anything else. But the world's supply of fresh, usable water is shrinking - both because of waste and because of climate-driven drought.

### The deeper picture
<cite index="19-1">According to the World Bank's first-ever Global Water Monitoring Report (released November 2025), the world is losing 324 billion cubic meters of freshwater every year, enough to meet the needs of 280 million people annually driven by worsening droughts and unsustainable land and water practices, including poor pricing policies, weak coordination, deforestation, wetland degradation and excessive irrigation.</cite>

FAO's own water-tracking system tells a similar story. <cite index="22-1">Renewable water availability per person has declined by 7 percent over the past decade, according to the 2025 AQUASTAT data snapshot, with pressure on already scarce freshwater resources increasing in several regions.</cite> <cite index="25-1">Regions such as Northern Africa, Southern Asia and Western Asia now have the lowest freshwater resources per capita, with Kuwait, the United Arab Emirates, Qatar and Yemen ranking among the countries with the least total renewable water.</cite>

The scale of dependence on water in farming is enormous. <cite index="23-1">Agriculture accounts for about 70% of all global water withdrawals, constantly competing with domestic, industrial and environmental needs for a limited supply.</cite> Yet irrigation is unevenly distributed: <cite index="26-1">today, 80% of global agriculture is rainfed, producing 60% of the world's food, but leaving 500 million smallholder farmers highly vulnerable to erratic rainfall and limited access to inputs and finance. Irrigated agriculture, by contrast, covers only 20% of farmland yet produces 40% of the world's food and in water-scarce areas, irrigation typically doubles yields compared to rainfed farming.</cite>

This gap is especially severe in the world's poorest farming regions. <cite index="26-1">In Sub-Saharan Africa, only 6% of farmland is irrigated, far below what's needed to meet rising food demand-even though irrigation can reduce childhood stunting by up to 20% and boost agricultural GDP by 46% in the region.</cite> With <cite index="26-1">global food demand expected to rise by more than 70% by 2050, the pressure on already limited water resources is intensifying as climate change, urbanization and industrial growth compete for the same shrinking supply.</cite>

### Why this matters
Water scarcity does not affect all farmers equally. It hits smallholders, the people with the least ability to dig deeper wells or build private irrigation systems the hardest, deepening global inequality in food production.

## Problem 3: Climate Change - Heat, Drought and Unpredictability Are Cutting Into Yields

### Simple explanation
Crops are adapted to certain temperature and rainfall patterns. When the climate shifts faster than farmers or crop varieties can adapt, yields fall even with modern technology and farming techniques trying to offset the damage.

### The deeper picture
This is one of the most rigorously studied problems in agriculture and recent large-scale research confirms the damage is real and is intensifying. A major 2025 study from the University of Illinois and the Climate Impact Lab found that <cite index="14-1">climate change is cutting global crop yields even when farmers adapt their practices, with the U.S. Midwest; a region especially well suited to today's corn and soybean production expected to be hit particularly hard under a high-warming future.</cite>

The damage is not evenly spread across the world. A 2025 peer-reviewed review found that <cite index="15-1">in Sub-Saharan Africa, staple crop yields are projected to decline by 10% to 20% by 2050 under current climate trends, threatening both food security and rural economies; in Ethiopia specifically, maize yields may fall by around 15% by 2050 due to rising temperatures and erratic rainfall.</cite>

Extreme weather events are also becoming a direct, visible threat to crops in real time. Carbon Brief's 2025 media analysis, cross-checked against FAO data, tracked <cite index="10-1">cases of crops being damaged by extreme weather worldwide between January 2023 and December 2025 and noted that the IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report found "sudden food production losses" due to extreme weather have become increasingly frequent since at least the mid-20th century, with climate models now projecting that yield impacts on major crops will occur sooner than previously expected; raising the risk of simultaneous failures across multiple global "breadbasket" regions at once.</cite>

It's worth noting there is genuine scientific debate about *how much* of this is offset by technology. Research published in *Nature Reports* and reviewed by Our World in Data shows a more nuanced picture: <cite index="17-1">crop yields have increased dramatically in recent decades even as climate change has slowed the productivity gains of key crops such as maize and soybeans though it may have had small positive effects on wheat in some regions.</cite> In other words, technology and better farming practices are still winning the race against climate change for now but the margin is shrinking and the IPCC's own modelling assumes continuing large gains in agricultural technology to keep pace.

### Why this matters
Climate change doesn't act alone - it multiplies almost every other problem in this report: it worsens water scarcity, expands the range of pests and diseases and adds financial risk that pushes smallholder farmers further from being able to get loans or insurance.

## Problem 4: Pests, Diseases and Invasive Species - A Moving Target

### Simple explanation
Warmer temperatures let insects and plant diseases survive in places that used to be too cold for them and let them reproduce faster where they already exist. Farmers are now fighting threats that didn't used to reach their fields.

### The deeper picture
The financial scale of this problem is significant. <cite index="55-1">Agricultural pests and diseases are estimated to cause over $220 billion in crop losses worldwide every year; a figure that underlines the scale of the challenge and the urgent need for better management, particularly in developing countries where these threats can tip communities from food self-sufficiency into full-blown food crises.</cite> Independent peer-reviewed estimates put the general scale of damage even higher: <cite index="57-1">pest and disease outbreaks cause an average of 10% to 30% of crop yield losses globally every year and climate change is increasing this risk further, as rising temperatures in temperate regions allow greater pest reproduction, wider geographic spread and higher overwintering survival rates.</cite>

This isn't theoretical - scientists have documented specific cases of pests physically moving to new territory as the climate warms. Research on the **Orange Wheat Blossom Midge**, a major wheat pest, found clear <cite index="61-1">latitudinal range shifts consistent with global warming, providing rare, unequivocal evidence connecting a real-world pest outbreak directly to climate change.</cite>

The problem compounds with how modern farming itself is structured: <cite index="56-1">warmer temperatures and shifting weather patterns favor resistant pest populations and help invasive species spread faster, while international trade and monoculture farming further increase vulnerability to outbreaks and resistance to chemical pesticides is rising, making traditional pest control less effective over time.</cite> Regional studies show the same pattern repeating around the world; in East Africa, for example, <cite index="58-1">rising temperatures, erratic rainfall and more frequent extreme weather are creating conditions that favor the spread of plant diseases and insect pests, jeopardizing food security in a region highly reliant on rain-fed farming.</cite>

### Why this matters
Pest and disease pressure turns farming into a moving target. A crop variety or pesticide that worked reliably for decades can suddenly stop working as the pest population shifts or evolves resistance; leaving farmers, especially poorer ones without access to updated seed varieties or crop protection tools, exposed.

## Problem 5: Pollinator Decline - A Quiet Threat to Nutrition, Not Just Yield

### Simple explanation
Many of the most nutritious foods - fruits, vegetables, nuts depend on bees and other pollinators to grow. Fewer pollinators means less of these foods, which pushes up prices and can worsen malnutrition, not just farm income.

### The deeper picture
The scale of dependence is larger than most people realize. <cite index="45-1">Approximately 87.5% of flowering plants are pollinated by animals and about 75% of global food crop types depend on pollinators meaning that without pollination, many interconnected ecosystem processes would collapse.</cite>

A landmark 2025 study published in *Nature Communications* modeled what would happen if wild pollinators collapsed in Europe by 2030 and the results were stark: <cite index="39-1">a hypothetical collapse of wild pollinators in Europe would reduce European crop yields by 8%, trigger cropland expansion and diminish net exports and while some market adjustments through land use and trade changes would partially soften the blow, they would risk worsening food insecurity and undermining biodiversity conservation efforts globally.</cite> <cite index="39-1">Global annual welfare losses from this scenario alone are estimated at €34 billion by 2030.</cite>

A broader global economic model, published in *Ecological Economics* in 2025, quantified the worldwide impact of a full pollinator collapse: <cite index="38-1">crop prices would rise by 30%, causing a global welfare loss of $729 billion USD equal to 0.9% of global GDP and 15.6% of the global agricultural production value used for human food while global Vitamin A availability would fall by 8%.</cite>

Perhaps most striking is the direct human-health finding from a 2025 global analysis: <cite index="43-1">inadequate wild pollination is already contributing to approximately 500,000 early deaths annually worldwide, because it reduces the supply of nutrient-rich foods like fruits, vegetables and nuts that are essential for preventing chronic disease.</cite> The causes of pollinator decline are well documented: <cite index="43-1">habitat destruction, widespread pesticide use particularly neonicotinoids, which impair insect navigation and reproduction even at low doses and the destabilizing effects of climate change.</cite>

### Why this matters
This problem is often overlooked because it doesn't show up as a dramatic crop failure; it shows up slowly, as rising prices for fruits and vegetables and gradually worsening nutrition, especially for lower-income households that rely on affordable produce.

## Problem 6: Labor Shortages and an Aging Farming Workforce

### Simple explanation
Fewer young people want to become farmers or farmworkers. The people currently farming are getting older, and there aren't enough new workers young or migrant; replacing them.

### The deeper picture
The numbers illustrate a genuinely global trend, not just a U.S. or European issue.

**United States:** <cite index="29-1">According to the 2022 agricultural census, the average age of the U.S. farmer is 58.1 years old and has been steadily rising since 2002; there are now four times as many farm producers aged 65 or older as there are producers younger than 35.</cite> <cite index="33-1">There were an estimated 2.4 million open agricultural jobs in the United States in 2024, with 56% of farmers reporting labor shortages and labor costs surged 17% in 2023 alone.</cite>

**United Kingdom and Canada:** <cite index="34-1">The average age of a farmer in the United Kingdom rose to 60 years by 2016 and 25% of Canadian farmers are projected to be 65 or older by 2025.</cite>

**European Union:** <cite index="34-1">About 2.5 million workers left the agricultural sector across the European Union over the past decade, associated with a declining number of farms and larger farm holdings and the agricultural workforce is forecast to keep shrinking by roughly 2% annually through 2030.</cite>

The underlying causes are consistent across regions: <cite index="34-1">a "brain drain" of young talent moving from rural to urban areas has been observed in many regions and countries, alongside growing concern over the low attractiveness of agricultural careers for young, qualified people.</cite> In the U.S. specifically, <cite index="28-1">the number of young, recent immigrants working in agriculture has fallen, causing the overall farm workforce to age between 2006 and 2022, the average age of foreign-born farmworkers alone rose by close to 7 years.</cite>

The consequences are already visible on the ground: <cite index="36-1">between 2017 and 2022, the United States lost 141,733 farms; a 7% decrease in just half a decade and in states like California, labor shortages have led directly to unharvested produce rotting in fields because farmers could not find enough workers to pick their crops.</cite>

### Why this matters
This is a demographic problem and demographic problems are slow to fix. Even with strong policy action today, it takes years to rebuild a skilled agricultural workforce; meaning today's labor gaps will likely shape food production and prices for at least a decade.

## Problem 7: Financial Exclusion and Post-Harvest Losses - Losing Food and Money Before It Reaches the Market

### Simple explanation
Even when a farmer successfully grows a good crop, two things can still take away their income: they might not have proper storage, so the crop spoils (post-harvest loss); and they usually can't get a loan to invest in better equipment, storage or seeds in the first place (financial exclusion). These two problems reinforce each other in a vicious cycle.

### The deeper picture
**Post-harvest losses** are staggering in scale. <cite index="53-1">Globally, 14% of all food - valued at an estimated $400 billion USD is lost between harvest and retail, with another 17% wasted at the retail and consumer level.</cite> In the world's poorest farming regions, the losses are even more severe. In Zimbabwe, for example, <cite index="47-1">about 30% of harvested grain is lost due to a lack of appropriate storage facilities, forcing farmers to sell most of their grain immediately after harvest at very low prices leaving them poor and vulnerable despite having grown a successful crop.</cite> Across West Africa more broadly, meta-analyses show that <cite index="49-1">smallholder farmers lose between 30% and 50% of their production annually to physical spoilage, quality degradation and market failures driven by inadequate storage infrastructure, poor temperature and humidity management, delayed threshing and drying, pest and fungal contamination and distance from processing facilities.</cite>

**Financial exclusion** compounds this problem directly. <cite index="48-1">There are an estimated 450 to 500 million smallholder farmers in the world, most of them in Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa, where they make up 90% of all farmers and produce 80% of the continent's food yet most of these farmers lack the basic financing needed to access inputs like fertilizer, seeds and tools and they remain frequently isolated from markets due to a lack of infrastructure that would let them transport goods to buyers.</cite>

Storage solutions exist, but cost is a major barrier: <cite index="50-1">cold storage facilities require high up-front investment and ongoing operating costs, making them largely inaccessible in developing countries due to limited access to finance, while metal silos; a cheaper alternative still carry high initial costs that discourage adoption by individual smallholders, though shared community-level silos can lower the cost per farmer.</cite>

There are encouraging signs of innovation, however. FAO-supported research in 2025 found that <cite index="54-1">flexible insurance premium payments tied to harvest seasons resulted in higher adoption rates of agricultural insurance in Kenya by helping farmers manage cash flow, while mobile savings accounts in Mozambique enabled farmers to accumulate funds and invest in fertilizer and community-based Village Savings and Loan Associations have proven particularly effective at helping rural members pool resources for credit and capital.</cite>

### Why this matters
This is arguably the most solvable problem in this report - it is less about physics (like climate or water) and more about infrastructure and financial systems. But without deliberate investment, it remains one of the biggest reasons why smallholder farmers stay trapped in poverty even when their harvests succeed.

## How These Problems Connect: A Root-Cause Map

These seven problems are not separate - they form a chain reaction:

```
Climate Change
   │
   ├──► Water Scarcity (droughts, changing rainfall) 
   │        │
   │        └──► Lower yields, especially for rainfed smallholder farmers
   │
   ├──► Land Degradation (heavier reliance on chemical inputs to compensate)
   │
   ├──► Pest & Disease Range Shifts (warming allows new outbreaks)
   │
   └──► Pollinator Decline (habitat loss + climate stress on insects)
            │
            └──► Reduced nutrition-crop yields → higher food prices

Labor Shortages ──► Higher production costs ──► Less investment in soil/water conservation

Financial Exclusion ──► Can't afford storage ──► Post-Harvest Losses ──► Farmers stay poor 
                                                                              │
                                                                              └──► Less able to invest in adapting to climate change
```

Notice the loop: farmers who are financially excluded cannot invest in the very technologies (irrigation, storage, resilient seeds) that would help them cope with climate change, water scarcity and pests; which keeps them poor. This is why experts consistently argue that **any single fix (e.g., better seeds alone) is not enough**; solutions need to address multiple links in this chain at once.

## Final Takeaway

Agriculture today faces a set of problems that are **environmental, economic and demographic all at once**. It is not simply a story of "farmers need better technology"; technology helps, but the deeper issues are structural: degraded soil, shrinking water supplies, an aging workforce and a lack of affordable finance and storage for the world's smallest and most vulnerable farmers. Solving world hunger and stabilizing food prices in the coming decades will depend less on any single innovation and more on addressing this entire interconnected system together.
